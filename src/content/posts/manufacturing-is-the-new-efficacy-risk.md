When I read a pitch deck, I start with the data. I look at the endpoints, the effect size and the comparator, and only then at the story. This month made me think the order needs one more step: **after the data, read the manufacturing plan.**

Two of September's biggest stories had the same subplot.

**FAYUVI.** Ultragenyx's gene therapy for Sanfilippo syndrome type A was [approved on September 17](/posts/fayuvi-sanfilippo-gene-therapy-approval/). The first application was rejected in July 2025, and the issue wasn't efficacy. The letter cited chemistry, manufacturing and controls (CMC) information and facility inspection findings. The same clinical data that was ready in 2025 reached patients a little over a year later.

**ITM-11.** On August 7, the FDA rejected ITM's radioligand therapy for neuroendocrine tumors. ITM said the agency found no issues with the clinical data, nonclinical data or safety, and cited CMC and a *third-party* commercial facility instead. Six weeks later, ITM [agreed to merge into Telix](/posts/telix-itm-radiopharma-merger/), and $100 million of the price depends on getting that approval by the end of 2027.

## Why this is happening

The modalities attracting capital right now, including gene therapy, cell therapy and radiopharmaceuticals, are ones where **the process is the product**. For a small molecule, you can hand a validated synthesis route to a contract manufacturer. For an AAV vector or a short-lived isotope, small changes in the process can change what ends up in the vial, and much of the capacity sits with a small number of specialized third parties.

Rejections like these don't make a drug less effective. They do cost time, runway and leverage. ITM negotiated its sale while fixing a CRL, which is a weaker position than negotiating with an approval in hand.

## What I'd ask any company in these modalities

1. **Who makes it, and who else depends on that facility?** A third-party site that has never passed a pre-approval inspection is a risk, just as a single-site Phase 3 is.
2. **Has the commercial process been used to make clinical material?** If there's a gap between how the trial drug was made and how the commercial drug will be made, find out how comparability will be shown.
3. **What does a CRL cost?** Model a 12–18 month delay. If the runway can't survive one manufacturing-driven rejection, that's a financing risk, not just a regulatory one.

## The investor takeaway

Clinical risk is usually priced carefully. Manufacturing risk is often assumed away until it arrives in a CRL. The Telix–ITM terms show one way to handle it: put part of the price behind milestones so the seller shares the regulatory risk. For earlier-stage investors, the lesson is simpler. **Diligence the supply chain as seriously as the science.**
